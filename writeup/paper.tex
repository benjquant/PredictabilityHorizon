\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Lyapunov exponents and \texorpdfstring{$\lambda_1$}{lambda1}}\label{sec:bg-lyapunov}
% Crystal clear: separation rate of nearby trajectories; the spectrum; lambda_1 = largest;
% sign(lambda_1) -> chaos; 1/lambda_1 = Lyapunov (predictability) time; how it is computed in
% principle (Benettin/QR: evolve a tangent frame, QR-orthonormalise, average log of R diagonals;
% Oseledets guarantees convergence).


Chaos is a concept borrowed from information theory, which quantifies how inherently difficult it is to make predictions about the future of dynamical systems. Mathematically speaking, a hallmark of chaos is the exponential divergence of nearby trajectories:

\begin{equation}
 x(t) - \tilde{x}(t) \sim e^{\lambda t}\;,
\end{equation}
where $\tilde x(t)=x(t)+\delta(t)$ is related to the original trajectory by a small pertubation $\delta(t)$.
The exponential rate of growth is called the "Lyapunov exponent". Goal of this work is to demonstrate that the Lyapunov exponent plays an important role in characterising the predictability horizons in robotic learning. 


To obtain a language that is more closely related to the robotics framework we want to study, it is advantageous to work in the tangent space picture. In practice, the trajectory $x(t)$ is determined by an ODE $\dot{x}=f(x)$, for some function $f(x(t))$. We can linearise the ODE for a perturbation $x+\delta$, and arrive at the expression
\begin{equation}
\dot{\delta}= D\!f(x)\,\delta\;, 
\end{equation}
with $D\!f(x)$ the Jacobian matrix.
Mirroring the mechanics of a differentiable simulator, we discretise the flow with a fixed step $dt$. This gives a one-step map $x_{t+1}=F(x_t)$, whose linearisation propagates the perturbation by the one-step Jacobian $J_t$:
\begin{equation}
\delta_{t+1} = J_t \delta_t, \qquad J_t = \frac{\partial x_{t+1}}{\partial x_t}.
\end{equation}

Solving the discrete recursion gives $\delta_t = M_t\,\delta_0$, propagated by the \emph{rollout Jacobian}
\begin{equation}
M_t = \frac{\partial x_t}{\partial x_0} = \prod_{s<t} J_s ,
\label{eq:rollout-jacobian}
\end{equation}
the fundamental matrix of the linearised flow. The \emph{largest Lyapunov exponent} is the asymptotic growth rate of a generic infinitesimal perturbation,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\lambda_1 = \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\,\ln\frac{\lVert M_t\,\delta_0\rVert}{\lVert\delta_0\rVert}\;},
\label{eq:lambda1}
\end{equation}
a limit that exists for almost every initial condition by Oseledets' multiplicative ergodic theorem~\cite{oseledets1968multiplicative}. The order of limits matters---$\delta_0$ infinitesimal first, then $t\to\infty$---which is why the exponent is a property of the linearised flow rather than of two finite trajectories, whose separation saturates once it is no longer small. A positive $\lambda_1$ is the operational signature of chaos: an initial uncertainty $\varepsilon$ grows to order unity within the \emph{predictability} (or \emph{Lyapunov}) \emph{time} $\tau \sim \lambda_1^{-1}\ln(1/\varepsilon)$, so $1/\lambda_1$ sets the horizon beyond which the trajectory is effectively unpredictable. A central claim of this work is that the \emph{same} $\lambda_1$ governs the predictability horizon of both computational objects of modern robot learning---differentiable simulators (Part~A) and learned world models (Part~B).

The perturbation directions that grow fastest are the leading singular vectors of $M_t$; its singular values $\sigma_i(M_t)\sim e^{\lambda_i t}$ define the full \emph{Lyapunov spectrum} $\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\dots\ge\lambda_n$. (Singular rather than eigenvalues: $M_t$ is non-normal, and a pure shear has unit eigenvalues yet still stretches space.) The sum of the spectrum measures the mean exponential growth of phase-space volume~\cite{eckmann1985ergodic},
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i = \big\langle \nabla\!\cdot f\big\rangle = \frac{1}{dt}\,\big\langle \ln\lvert\det J\rvert\big\rangle,
\label{eq:sum-rule}
\end{equation}
the time-averaged divergence of the flow. The systems we study are Hamiltonian, so Liouville's theorem gives $\nabla\!\cdot f = 0$ and the spectrum sums to zero: phase-space volume is conserved. This volume-preservation constraint is a structural fingerprint that a faithful model must reproduce---a theme we return to in Part~B (Section~\ref{sec:partB})---and serves throughout as a consistency check on the estimator.

Reading the \emph{full spectrum} off an explicitly formed $M_t$ is numerically hopeless: the dynamic range $\sigma_1/\sigma_n\sim e^{(\lambda_1-\lambda_n)t}$ over- or under-flows and every column collapses onto the dominant direction, burying the sub-dominant exponents within a few thousand steps. We instead use the standard Benettin/QR estimator~\cite{benettin1980lyapunov,engelken2023lyapunov}: evolve an orthonormal frame $Q$ under the one-step Jacobians, re-orthonormalise at each step through a factorisation $J_t Q = Q'R$, and accumulate the logarithms of the diagonal entries of $R$; the running averages converge to the exponents $\lambda_i$ (reported per unit time, after dividing by $dt$), with convergence guaranteed by Oseledets. A single such estimator is used throughout, calibrated against known answers: it returns zero to machine precision on a harmonic oscillator and recovers $\lambda_1\approx 0.906$ for the Lorenz system, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:m-lyapunov}.


\subsection{Jacobians and differentiable simulation}\label{sec:bg-jacobian}
% One-step Jacobian J_t = d x_{t+1}/d x_t; rollout Jacobian M_T = prod_t J_t = d x_T/d x_0; why it
% is THE object a differentiable simulator backprops (system ID, trajectory optimisation, first-order
% policy gradients all go through the rollout); the bridge ||M_T|| ~ e^{lambda_1 T}.

A \emph{differentiable simulator} is a physics engine whose one-step map $x_{t+1}=F(x_t)$ is implemented so that derivatives propagate through it: applying reverse-mode automatic differentiation to a rollout returns the gradient of a trajectory objective with respect to the initial state, the physical parameters, or a sequence of control inputs. We use NVIDIA Warp~\cite{macklin2022warp} for every simulator in this work; the implementation details are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:m-warp}.

The object such a simulator differentiates is exactly the rollout Jacobian of Section~\ref{sec:bg-lyapunov}. For a scalar loss $L(x_T)$ evaluated at the end of a $T$-step rollout, the chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_0} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial x_T}\,M_T, \qquad M_T = \prod_{t<T} J_t,
\label{eq:loss-grad}
\end{equation}
with $M_T$ the rollout Jacobian of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rollout-jacobian}. Reverse-mode differentiation evaluates this product right-to-left, multiplying in one $J_t$ per step as it walks back along the stored trajectory. Every gradient a differentiable simulator produces is therefore a product of one-step Jacobians---the same product that governs the growth of perturbations.

This is not a peculiarity of one loss. The three canonical uses of a differentiable simulator all backpropagate through the rollout and so all carry the factor $M_T$: system identification differentiates the trajectory with respect to physical parameters, trajectory optimisation differentiates it with respect to a control sequence, and first-order (analytic) policy gradients differentiate it with respect to policy parameters~\cite{suh2022differentiable,parmas2018pipps}. In each case the gradient inherits whatever $M_T$ does.

What $M_T$ does is set by $\lambda_1$. From the definition of the largest Lyapunov exponent, Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda1}, the rollout Jacobian stretches a generic vector as $\norm{M_T}\sim e^{\lambda_1 T}$, so on a chaotic system ($\lambda_1>0$) the gradient magnitude grows exponentially in the horizon. The useful signal does not grow with it: the exponentially large contributions come from the most sensitive directions and inflate both the gradient and its variance, so beyond $T\sim 1/\lambda_1$ the analytic gradient is dominated by chaotic amplification rather than by the quantity one meant to optimise~\cite{metz2021gradients,parmas2018pipps,suh2022differentiable}. This is the \emph{curse of chaos} for differentiable simulators, and it is the precise statement Part~A measures: the same $1/\lambda_1$ horizon that bounds prediction also bounds the useful length of a differentiable rollout.

Equation~\eqref{eq:loss-grad} also gives a second, independent handle on $\lambda_1$. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda1}, the growth rate of the gradient gain $\norm{M_T}$ with $T$ is itself a finite-horizon estimate of $\lambda_1$---distinct from the Benettin/QR estimator of Section~\ref{sec:bg-lyapunov}, computed from the same orbit, and expected to agree with it as $T\to\infty$ by Oseledets. Section~\ref{sec:m-consistency} makes this comparison the basis of Part~A.

\subsection{The two systems}\label{sec:bg-systems}
% pendulum (integrable, lambda_1 = 0), acrobot (chaotic, lambda_1 > 0).
% Characterised as ODE / Hamiltonian systems; separable vs non-separable Hamiltonian; discretised by
% semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler.

We study two mechanical systems that share a common structure but span the range of dynamical behaviour we care about. Each is a conservative Hamiltonian system: writing the state as a configuration $q$ with conjugate momentum $p$, the dynamics follow from a Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
H(q,p) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,p^\top M(q)^{-1} p + V(q),
\label{eq:hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
the sum of kinetic and potential energy, with $M(q)$ the configuration-dependent mass (inertia) matrix~\cite{arnold1989mathematical}. Equivalently, in second-order form the passive dynamics obey the manipulator equation $M(q)\ddot q + C(q,\dot q)\dot q + G(q) = 0$~\cite{tedrake2023underactuated}; control enters as a forcing term on the right, but our Lyapunov diagnostics concern the underlying conservative flow, for which Liouville's theorem applies and the spectrum sums to zero as in Section~\ref{sec:bg-lyapunov}.

The two systems form a deliberate contrast in $\lambda_1$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pendulum} --- a single degree of freedom with separable Hamiltonian $H=\tfrac{1}{2}p^2 + V(\theta)$. A one-degree-of-freedom autonomous Hamiltonian is integrable: energy conservation confines every orbit to a level set of $H$, leaving no room for exponential divergence, so $\lambda_1=0$~\cite{arnold1989mathematical}. This is our ordered baseline.
\item \textbf{Acrobot} --- a two-link pendulum actuated only at the elbow, the textbook chaotic underactuated system~\cite{spong1995swing}. Its inertia matrix $M(q)$ depends on the configuration, making the Hamiltonian \emph{non-separable}, and its passive dynamics are chaotic, $\lambda_1>0$.
\end{itemize}

The separable/non-separable distinction is the structural divide. When $M$ is constant the kinetic term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hamiltonian} decouples from $q$, and the position and momentum updates can be taken independently; when $M(q)$ varies---as for the acrobot---the two are coupled. That coupling is both the origin of the acrobot's chaos and the reason it is the demanding case for the structure-preserving integrators and world models of Part~B.

Because the consistency checks of Section~\ref{sec:bg-lyapunov} rely on volume preservation, the integrator must not manufacture or destroy phase-space volume of its own accord. We therefore discretise with semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler. For the two conservative systems whose Lyapunov spectrum we measure this preserves the symplectic structure and keeps the discrete one-step map volume-preserving with $\sum_i\lambda_i=0$~\cite{hairer2006geometric,leimkuhler2004simulating}---exactly for the separable pendulum ($\det J=1$ to machine precision), and up to $O(dt^2)$ for the non-separable acrobot---whereas a generic explicit scheme (forward Euler, standard Runge--Kutta) injects numerical dissipation or expansion that would bias the very spectrum we are trying to measure. The step kernels are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:m-warp}. The resulting ladder---$\lambda_1=0$ for the pendulum up to $\lambda_1>0$ for the acrobot---is the controlled axis along which Part~A sweeps the gradient diagnostics and Part~B contrasts a learned model's recovered exponent against the truth.

\section{Methods --- what we simulate and how}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Differentiable Warp simulators}\label{sec:m-warp}
% semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler step kernels; rollout stored as a (T+1, dim) tape (never
% overwriting a cell) so reverse-mode adjoints through the trajectory are correct; CPU, single-thread.

We implement each system of Section~\ref{sec:bg-systems} directly as a Warp~\cite{macklin2022warp}
compute kernel that advances the state by one \emph{velocity-first semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler} step:
the angular acceleration is evaluated at the current state, the velocity is updated first, and the
configuration is then advanced with the \emph{new} velocity,
\begin{equation}
\omega_{t+1} = \omega_t + dt\,a(q_t,\omega_t), \qquad
q_{t+1} = q_t + dt\,\omega_{t+1}.
\label{eq:semi-implicit}
\end{equation}
For a single degree of freedom this map is exactly symplectic; for the non-separable acrobot it is
symplectic to $O(dt^2)$ (Section~\ref{sec:bg-systems}). The accelerations $a$ are the only
system-specific part:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pendulum} (state $(\theta,\omega)$): $a = -(g/\ell)\sin\theta$.
\item \textbf{Acrobot} (state $(\theta_1,\theta_2,\omega_1,\omega_2)$): the angular accelerations
solve the manipulator equation $M(\theta)\,\dot\omega = F(\theta,\omega)$ with the point-mass
double-pendulum inertia matrix and passive (Coriolis + gravity) forcing
\begin{equation}
M(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix} (m_1+m_2)\,l_1^2 & m_2 l_1 l_2 \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\\[2pt]
m_2 l_1 l_2 \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) & m_2 l_2^2 \end{bmatrix},\quad
F=\begin{bmatrix} -m_2 l_1 l_2\,\omega_2^2 \sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) - (m_1+m_2)\,g l_1 \sin\theta_1\\[2pt]
\phantom{-}m_2 l_1 l_2\,\omega_1^2 \sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) - m_2 g l_2 \sin\theta_2 \end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:acrobot-massmatrix}
\end{equation}
The kernel advances Eq.~\eqref{eq:semi-implicit} with $\dot\omega = M(\theta)^{-1}F$ in the closed
form obtained by inverting the $2\times2$ system; we verified that this closed form reproduces
$M(\theta)^{-1}F$ to machine precision. Because $M$ depends on $\theta$, the Hamiltonian is
non-separable, which is the structural feature exploited in Part~B (Section~\ref{sec:partB}).
\end{itemize}

Reverse-mode differentiation requires the full trajectory to be available on the backward pass, so
each rollout is stored as a $(T{+}1)\times\dim$ array, writing one new row per step and never
overwriting a cell; the adjoint then walks back along the stored states, multiplying in one
one-step Jacobian $J_t$ per step exactly as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss-grad}. All simulators run on CPU,
single-threaded. The integration step and physical parameters are collected in
Table~\ref{tab:sim-params}.

\subsection{The Lyapunov estimator and its calibration}\label{sec:m-lyapunov}
% Our single Benettin/QR estimator; calibration against known answers (harmonic oscillator 0;
% Lorenz ~0.906); per-step Jacobian cross-checks (Warp autodiff vs analytic / finite-difference).

We operationalize the Benettin/QR estimator of Section~\ref{sec:bg-lyapunov} as follows. From a
seeded random orthonormal frame $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$ (the $k$ leading exponents; $k=n$ for
the full spectrum), we advance the tangent frame one step at a time, re-orthonormalising through a
thin QR factorisation $J_t Q = Q'R$ at \emph{every} step, accumulating $\ln\lvert R_{ii}\rvert$, and
dividing the running sums by the elapsed time $N\,dt$ to obtain the exponents in physical
(per-unit-time) units, sorted in descending order (the telescoping that makes
$\frac{1}{N\,dt}\sum_t \ln R^{(t)}_{ii}\to\lambda_i$ exact is spelled out in
Appendix~\ref{app:qr}). The initial transient is discarded by slicing the trajectory before the average: for the dissipative
Lorenz calibration this is the relaxation onto the strange attractor (the first $2000$ steps), and for
every system it is the alignment of the QR frame onto the leading Oseledets directions
(Section~\ref{sec:m-consistency}). A
single such estimator is used for every result in this paper.

We calibrate it against two systems with known answers.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Harmonic oscillator} (an exact rotation map, all exponents zero): the estimator
returns $\lvert\lambda_i\rvert \lesssim 2\times10^{-15}$ over $2\times10^4$ steps---zero to machine
precision, as expected since each step contributes $\ln\lvert{\pm}1\rvert = 0$ exactly.
\item \textbf{Lorenz} ($\sigma=10,\ \rho=28,\ \beta=8/3$; RK4 at $dt=5\times10^{-3}$, first $2000$
steps dropped): the finite-time largest exponent is itself a fluctuating quantity. Single-trajectory
estimates scatter by $\sim\!0.02$ around the standard literature value $\lambda_1 = 0.9056$ over a
few hundred Lyapunov times, and the running average on a long trajectory converges to it
($\lambda_1 = 0.905$ at $1.6\times10^3$ Lyapunov times). This scatter is intrinsic finite-time
variance, not a Jacobian or estimator bias: finite-difference and analytic (variational-RK4)
Jacobians agree on $\lambda_1$ to $3\times10^{-8}$ along a common orbit. As a sharp structural check
valid at \emph{every} horizon, the recovered spectrum has a near-zero middle exponent and sums to
$\sum_i\lambda_i = -13.667$, matching the constant flow divergence
$\operatorname{tr} Df = -(\sigma+1+\beta)$ to machine precision (Eq.~\eqref{eq:sum-rule}).
\end{itemize}

Finally, the per-step Jacobian feeding the estimator is computed analytically for the pendulum and
by central finite differences for the acrobot, and each is cross-checked against a third, independent
route---Warp's reverse-mode autodiff. The analytic pendulum Jacobian agrees with autodiff to
$1.7\times10^{-9}$ and the central-difference acrobot Jacobian to $2.0\times10^{-5}$, confirming that
the tangent dynamics the estimator integrates are the ones the simulator actually propagates.

\subsection{Two estimators of \texorpdfstring{$\lambda_1$}{lambda1}}\label{sec:m-consistency}
% gradient-gain slope (from the growth of ||M_T||) vs the Benettin lambda_1: two distinct finite-T
% functionals of the SAME orbit, expected to agree as T -> infinity (Oseledets). Sets up Part A.

Part~A rests on comparing two finite-horizon estimates of $\lambda_1$ computed from the \emph{same}
orbit. The
first is the calibrated Benettin/QR estimator of Section~\ref{sec:m-lyapunov}. The second reads
$\lambda_1$ off the growth of the gradient gain introduced in Section~\ref{sec:bg-jacobian}: since
$\norm{M_T}\sim e^{\lambda_1 T}$, the least-squares slope of $\ln\norm{M_T}_2$ against $T$ is itself
an estimate of $\lambda_1$. Here $\norm{M_T}_2$ is the spectral norm (top singular value) of the
rollout Jacobian, and the slope is fit over a range of horizons reaching several Lyapunov times
($T$ from $\sim\!10^3$ up to $1.6\times10^4$ integration steps).

These two numbers are distinct finite-$T$ functionals of the same Jacobian sequence, \emph{not}
algebraically identical: the QR estimator averages the local logarithmic stretching of an
orthonormal frame, whereas the slope tracks the global spectral norm of the accumulated product.
Both converge to the true $\lambda_1$ as $T\to\infty$ by Oseledets' theorem, but only once
$T \gg 1/(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)$---the time a generic direction needs to align with the leading one
---and the slope is the higher-variance of the two. Part~A therefore reports their agreement as a
\emph{consistency relation}---evidence that the gradient-gain growth rate and the dynamical
$\lambda_1$ are one and the same number---rather than as an exact identity.

\section{Part A --- \texorpdfstring{$\lambda_1$}{lambda1} bounds differentiable-simulator gradients}\label{sec:partA}
% ||M_T|| ~ e^{lambda_1 T} (one factor); slope = lambda_1 (exact on a linear map; consistent on the
% chaotic acrobot); regime sweep -> slope tracks lambda_1, corr 0.99 (Fig 5); the horizon T <~ 1/lambda_1
% demonstrated via the analytic-gradient SNR (Fig 6); acrobot trajectory optimisation, precise vs forgiving (Fig 2).
Part~A establishes the first pillar of the thesis: the largest Lyapunov exponent $\lambda_1$ governs
the gradients a differentiable simulator produces. Section~\ref{sec:bg-jacobian} showed that every
such gradient carries the rollout-Jacobian factor $M_T$ with $\norm{M_T}\sim e^{\lambda_1 T}$, and
Section~\ref{sec:m-consistency} set up a second, gradient-side estimate of $\lambda_1$ read off that
growth rate.

Two systems play distinct roles. The chaotic \emph{acrobot} is the workhorse and carries every
$\lambda_1$ claim; the integrable \emph{pendulum} ($\lambda_1\approx0$) is the null contrast, showing
the effect vanishes when the dynamics are not chaotic.

Against this cast we (i) confirm the growth law and that its rate \emph{is} $\lambda_1$, on the
matched pendulum--acrobot pair; (ii) show it holds across the whole integrable--chaotic range, by
sweeping the acrobot's energy so $\lambda_1$ climbs continuously from near zero; (iii) turn the
growth law into a \emph{usefulness} limit---the gradient predictability horizon $T\lesssim 1/\lambda_1$---through
the collapse of the analytic-gradient signal-to-noise ratio on the acrobot; and (iv) draw the
consequence for trajectory optimisation through the simulator, where the horizon bounds \emph{precise}
control on the acrobot but a forgiving swing-up sails through it.

\subsection{The gradient-gain growth law}\label{sec:A-law}
% slope of log||M_T|| = lambda_1; pendulum integrable (no blow-up) vs acrobot chaotic (e^{lambda_1 T}); Fig 1.
This subsection operationalises the bridge of Section~\ref{sec:bg-jacobian}---that a differentiable
simulator's gradient carries the factor $\norm{M_T}\sim e^{\lambda_1 T}$---and reads its rate off the
slope estimator of Section~\ref{sec:m-consistency}. For each system we plot $\ln\norm{M_T}_2$ against
the horizon $T$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:gradient-law}); the slope is the gradient-gain rate. On the chaotic
acrobot the gain grows exponentially at $1.07\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, matching the independently measured
Benettin $\lambda_1 = 1.18\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$; on the integrable pendulum both rates vanish
($\lambda_1\approx 0.08$, slope $\approx 0.06\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$) and the gain is sub-exponential. The
gradient gain thus blows up \emph{at the Lyapunov rate} on chaotic dynamics, and not at all on
integrable ones.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig1.png}
  \caption{Gradient gain $\norm{\partial x_T/\partial x_0}_2$ versus rollout horizon $T$ (semilog).
  Integrable pendulum (left): sub-exponential, $\lambda_1\approx 0$. Chaotic acrobot (right):
  exponential growth whose log-slope ($1.07\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$) matches the Benettin $\lambda_1$
  ($1.18\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$).}
  \label{fig:gradient-law}
\end{figure}

Two points fix the interpretation. First, the rate is a \emph{single} factor of $\lambda_1$: we fit
the Jacobian norm $\norm{M_T}_2$, which grows as $e^{\lambda_1 T}$, so its log-slope is $\lambda_1$;
a squared quantity---a squared-loss objective, or the squared separation $\norm{\delta x_T}^2$---would
grow as $e^{2\lambda_1 T}$ and read $2\lambda_1$ instead. Second, the coincidence of the two rates is
the \emph{consistency relation} of Section~\ref{sec:m-consistency}, not an identity: slope and Benettin
exponent are exact equals only for a constant-Jacobian map, and on the acrobot they agree to within the
finite-time scatter of the higher-variance slope ($\approx 0.1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, about $10\%$).

\subsection{The law holds across regimes}\label{sec:A-regimes}
% slope tracks Benettin lambda_1 across an acrobot energy sweep + pendulum, corr ~0.99; Fig 5.
Section~\ref{sec:A-law} fixed the law on two orbits; here we vary the dynamics continuously. We sweep
the acrobot across initial energies ($\theta_1$ from $0.5$ to $3.0$, raising $\lambda_1$ from near
zero to $\sim\!1.2\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$) and add the integrable pendulum, and for each we compare the
gradient-gain slope against the Benettin $\lambda_1$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:slope-vs-lambda}). The points
cluster on the line slope $=\lambda_1$: across the integrable-to-chaotic range the two rates correlate
at $0.99$, with best-fit slope $0.96$ (ideal $1$) and near-zero intercept. The gradient-gain rate is
thus $\lambda_1$ not merely on a single orbit but as a function of the regime.

  \caption{Gradient-gain slope versus Benettin $\lambda_1$ across an acrobot energy sweep

  Horizontal bars are finite-time Lyapunov window scatter, not confidence intervals.}

The acrobot energy sweep already spans the integrable-to-chaotic transition, so no further system is
needed here. The horizontal error bars
report the finite-time scatter of the Benettin estimate over independent windows---an honest depiction
of finite-time Lyapunov uncertainty, not a confidence interval on the plotted point.

\subsection{The gradient predictability horizon}\label{sec:A-horizon}
% analytic-gradient SNR collapses past T*lambda_1 ~ 1; genuine decorrelation; Fig 6.
The growth law has a sharp operational consequence. Section~\ref{sec:bg-jacobian} argued that because
$\norm{M_T}$ inflates both a gradient and its variance, beyond $T\sim 1/\lambda_1$ the analytic gradient
of a differentiable rollout is dominated by chaotic amplification rather than by the objective---the
\emph{curse of chaos}. We make this quantitative with a signal-to-noise ratio: perturbing the initial
state over an ensemble of nearby conditions, we compare the mean analytic gradient
$\nabla_{x_0}\norm{x_T}^2$ to its spread across the ensemble (Fig.~\ref{fig:gradient-horizon}). On the
acrobot ($\lambda_1=1.23\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$) the SNR is large well inside the horizon ($\approx\!170$ at
$T\lambda_1\approx0.3$) and falls by roughly an order of magnitude past $T\lambda_1\approx1$ (to
$\approx\!20$--$50$): the gradient still has a large magnitude, but its \emph{direction} is no longer
shared across nearby initial conditions. The crossover sits at the predictability horizon
$T\sim1/\lambda_1$, up to a logarithmic factor $\sim\log(1/\varepsilon)/(\lambda_1\,dt)$ set by the
perturbation scale $\varepsilon$.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{fig6.png}
  \caption{Analytic-gradient SNR versus Lyapunov time $T\lambda_1$ (acrobot). The SNR degrades past the
  predictability horizon $T\lambda_1=1$ (dashed): the gradient's direction decorrelates across nearby
  initial conditions even though its magnitude keeps growing.}
  \label{fig:gradient-horizon}
\end{figure}

This collapse is genuinely nonlinear, not a normalisation artifact. Each ensemble gradient has
magnitude $\sim\norm{M_T}\sim e^{\lambda_1 T}$, so the ensemble variance grows as $e^{2\lambda_1 T}$;
on a purely linear map this common growth cancels between the mean (signal) and the spread (noise) and
the SNR stays flat. The observed decline therefore measures real loss of \emph{directional} agreement
among neighbouring trajectories: past $T\sim1/\lambda_1$ the analytic gradient points a different way
for every nearby start, which is exactly what makes long differentiable rollouts unusable for
first-order optimisation.

\subsection{Consequence: optimisation through the simulator}\label{sec:A-consequence}
% acrobot trajectory optimisation: precise reach hits the log-factor wall, forgiving swing-up does not; Fig 2.
Sections~\ref{sec:A-law}--\ref{sec:A-horizon} measured how far a differentiable rollout's gradient
stays usable; here we draw the consequence for optimisation, and reach a statement sharper than
``the gradient works inside the horizon and fails outside it''. On the chaotic acrobot we optimise an
open-loop elbow torque by gradient descent through the differentiable Warp rollout (Adam), and track
each objective's \emph{normalized final cost}---the achieved cost as a fraction of the do-nothing
baseline---as the rollout horizon grows (Fig.~\ref{fig:trajopt}); the integrable pendulum is the null
control.

The gradient of a single deterministic rollout is \emph{exact}, so optimisation succeeds well beyond
the naive Lyapunov time. A \emph{forgiving} objective---swing the hand to upright, satisfied by a large
basin of control sequences---succeeds at every horizon we tried; and a \emph{precise} objective---reach
a specific feasible terminal state $x_T^\star$ (one a known control attains, so a zero-cost solution
provably exists)---succeeds out to several Lyapunov times. The horizon does not simply forbid
optimisation past $1/\lambda_1$.

The precise objective nonetheless hits a sharp wall, at exactly the horizon Section~\ref{sec:A-horizon}
predicts. Past the predictability horizon---$1/\lambda_1$ carrying the same logarithmic factor in the
achievable precision that set the SNR crossover, here $T\lambda_1\approx4.6$---the precise-reach cost
rises \emph{through} the do-nothing baseline: the optimiser ends worse than zero control, its
squared-error gradient inheriting the $e^{2\lambda_1 T}$ rollout gain until the descent diverges (to
$\sim\!300\times$ baseline by $T\lambda_1=5$). The wall is genuinely chaotic---the integrable pendulum,
optimised over identical horizons and step counts, stays orders of magnitude below the baseline
throughout, as does the forgiving swing-up on the same chaotic acrobot. It is specific both to chaos
and to objectives that demand precision.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{fig2.png}
  \caption{Optimisation through the differentiable simulator on the chaotic acrobot versus rollout
  horizon $T\lambda_1$. Normalized final cost (achieved cost / do-nothing baseline; geometric mean over
  reach targets, shaded $10$--$90\%$ band). A \emph{forgiving} objective (swing the hand up; green) and
  the \emph{integrable} pendulum's \emph{precise} target-reaching (blue) stay orders of magnitude below
  the do-nothing baseline (dotted) at every horizon, while the chaotic acrobot's \emph{precise}
  target-reaching (red) rises through the baseline near the predictability horizon $T\lambda_1\approx4.6$
  ($1/\lambda_1$ up to the logarithmic factor of Section~\ref{sec:A-horizon}) and blows up beyond it as
  the $e^{2\lambda_1 T}$ gradient gain makes the descent diverge; the widening red band marks the loss
  of reliability. The horizon bounds \emph{precise} differentiable-sim control; forgiving objectives
  and integrable dynamics show no wall.}
  \label{fig:trajopt}
\end{figure}

The consequence is a refined curse of chaos for differentiable
simulation~\cite{suh2022differentiable}. The predictability horizon---with its logarithmic
factor---bounds \emph{precise} control through a differentiable simulator: when the objective pins a
specific terminal state, the chaotically amplified gradient can no longer deliver it past
$T\sim\log(1/\tau)/\lambda_1$. It does not bound differentiable-sim optimisation in general---an exact
single-trajectory gradient solves a forgiving objective at any horizon, and an integrable system has no
wall. This is the operational face of the signal-to-noise collapse of Section~\ref{sec:A-horizon},
whose squared-norm objective is itself a precise one; the wall here is for open-loop single-shooting,
and multiple shooting over sub-horizon segments would push it out.

\section{Part B --- Lyapunov fidelity of learned world models}\label{sec:partB}
% MLP world model; prediction-error growth (Fig 3, qualitative); learned vs true lambda_1 (Fig 4:
% reproduces the integrable exponent, over-amplifies the chaotic one); mechanism (next-step MSE does
% not constrain the Jacobian / breaks volume preservation); structure-preserving sequel (Fig 7):
% plain MLP / volume-penalty MLP / symplectic HNN -- only the hard constraint recovers lambda_1.

Part~A established that the largest Lyapunov exponent $\lambda_1$ governs the gradients a
differentiable simulator produces when the physics is correct. Part~B asks the dual question for a
model that is \emph{learned} rather than given: does a world model trained only to predict the next
state inherit the right $\lambda_1$? This is the spine of the section. A multilayer perceptron fit by
next-step regression reproduces the integrable exponent but \emph{over-amplifies} the chaotic
one---because next-step accuracy never constrains the Jacobian that carries $\lambda_1$---and only a
model built with the right \emph{hard} structural constraint, symplectic and volume-preserving,
recovers it.

We reuse the cast of Part~A: the integrable \emph{pendulum} as the null contrast and the chaotic
\emph{acrobot} as the workhorse.
The argument runs in four steps: the learned model and how fast its rollout drifts from the truth
(Section~\ref{sec:B-model}); the learned-versus-true $\lambda_1$ that its Jacobian implies
(Section~\ref{sec:B-lyap}); why next-step training is blind to $\lambda_1$ in the first place
(Section~\ref{sec:B-mechanism}); and which structural constraint repairs it
(Section~\ref{sec:B-structure}).

\subsection{The learned world model and its prediction drift}\label{sec:B-model}

A world model replaces the simulator's one-step map $x_{t+1}=F(x_t)$ with a learned surrogate
$g_\phi$, fit so that iterating it reproduces trajectories of the true system. We deliberately use the
smallest model that exhibits the effect: a residual multilayer perceptron
$g_\phi(x)=x+\mathrm{net}_\phi(x)$ with two $\tanh$ hidden layers, trained by next-step mean-squared
error on rollouts of the true simulator. It is given only the state pairs $(x_t,x_{t+1})$---no
equations of motion, no Hamiltonian, no conserved quantity---which is exactly the regime in which one
hopes a generic learner will simply absorb the dynamics from data.

Such a model can be accurate one step at a time yet useless over a rollout, so the first question is
how quickly its predictions drift. We roll $g_\phi$ forward from a shared initial condition and track
the prediction error $\norm{\hat x_t - x_t}$ against the true trajectory (Fig.~\ref{fig:error-growth}).
On both systems the error grows, but at visibly different rates: on the integrable pendulum it rises
slowly, while on the chaotic acrobot it climbs roughly twice as fast before saturating. A log-linear
fit over the pre-saturation window gives effective rates of $0.96\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ (pendulum) and
$1.74\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ (acrobot).

These rates measure \emph{model fidelity}, not the Lyapunov exponent. Two effects are superimposed:
the model's own approximation error, which accumulates even on the non-chaotic pendulum, and---on the
acrobot---the genuine chaotic amplification of that error. The pendulum makes the distinction
concrete: its fitted $0.96\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ is nowhere near its true $\lambda_1\approx0$, so the drift
rate cannot be read as $\lambda_1$. Figure~\ref{fig:error-growth} is therefore a qualitative
statement---prediction degrades faster on chaotic dynamics---and isolating the exponent the model has
actually learned requires probing its Jacobian directly, the subject of Section~\ref{sec:B-lyap}.

  \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{fig3.png}
  \caption{Residual-MLP world-model prediction error $\norm{\hat x_t - x_t}$ versus physical time
  (semilog). Error grows on both systems but faster on the chaotic acrobot (effective log-slope
  $1.74\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$) than on the integrable pendulum ($0.96\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$). These are
  model-fidelity rates, not $\lambda_1$: the pendulum's is far from its true $\lambda_1\approx0$. The
  Lyapunov comparison is Section~\ref{sec:B-lyap}.}
  \label{fig:error-growth}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Learned versus true \texorpdfstring{$\lambda_1$}{lambda1}}\label{sec:B-lyap}

To read off the exponent the model has learned, we apply the \emph{same} Benettin/QR estimator used for
the true systems (Section~\ref{sec:m-lyapunov}) to the model's one-step Jacobian
$\partial g_\phi/\partial x$, obtained by automatic differentiation. The only subtlety is where to
evaluate it: rolling the model out and estimating $\lambda_1$ along its \emph{own} trajectory would
conflate an inaccurate Jacobian with a drifted orbit, so we instead evaluate the learned Jacobian along
the \emph{true} trajectory, after the same transient. Estimator, orbit, and transient are then
identical for the learned and reference exponents; only the source of the Jacobian differs---true
physics versus learned model---so any gap is the model's Jacobian alone. A faithful model, whose
Jacobians match the true ones along the orbit, would return the true $\lambda_1$.

Figure~\ref{fig:lyap-scatter} plots learned against true $\lambda_1$ for the two systems. On the
integrable pendulum the model lands on the truth: a learned $\lambda_1\approx 0.046\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$
against a true $0.063\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, both effectively zero---the network correctly reports
non-chaotic dynamics. On the chaotic acrobot it does not: the learned exponent is
$3.45\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ against a true $1.04\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, an over-amplification by a factor of
$3.3$. The model is not merely imprecise here; it manufactures sensitivity the true dynamics do not
have.

The asymmetry is the point. The network is trained to low next-step error on both systems, yet it
reproduces $\lambda_1$ only where the exponent is trivial and inflates it precisely where chaotic
sensitivity makes it matter. Low next-step error is therefore no guarantee of dynamical fidelity:
$\lambda_1$ is a property the training objective never sees, and on chaotic dynamics the learned model
overstates it threefold. Section~\ref{sec:B-mechanism} explains why next-step regression is blind to
it.

  \caption{Learned versus true $\lambda_1$, both from the same Benettin/QR estimator evaluated along
  the true orbit. The integrable pendulum sits on the $y=x$ line (both $\lambda_1\approx0$); the
  chaotic acrobot lies far above it---learned $3.45\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ versus true
  $1.04\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, an over-amplification of $3.3\times$. Next-step accuracy does not imply the
  right $\lambda_1$.}
  \label{fig:lyap-scatter}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Why next-step training cannot see \texorpdfstring{$\lambda_1$}{lambda1}}\label{sec:B-mechanism}

The failure in Section~\ref{sec:B-lyap} has a precise cause. Next-step mean-squared error penalises
$\norm{g_\phi(x_t)-x_{t+1}}^2$ at the training states: it supervises \emph{where} points are mapped.
But $\lambda_1$ is a property of the \emph{Jacobian} $\partial g_\phi/\partial x$, which governs how
infinitesimal perturbations are stretched, and next-step error never targets a derivative. With finite
data and finite capacity a model can drive its pointwise error low while its Jacobian---constrained by
no derivative target---remains substantially wrong. The threefold over-amplification of
Section~\ref{sec:B-lyap} is exactly that gap made visible: the network reproduces the trajectory well
yet equips it with the wrong sensitivity.

There is a sharper way to name what is missing. The true dynamics are Hamiltonian, and in canonical
coordinates they conserve phase-space volume exactly---the Lyapunov spectrum sums to zero
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:sum-rule}). That volume law is a structural fingerprint of genuine mechanics, yet it is a
property of the Jacobian's \emph{determinant}---once more, the object next-step regression never
touches---so a pointwise learner has no route to discover it. We read this fidelity off the
coordinate-invariant exponent $\lambda_1$ rather than a raw volume sum, since the volume law is exact
only in the canonical $(\theta,p)$ frame and not the $(\theta,\omega)$ coordinates the model is fit in.
The same reasoning points to the remedy: rather than hope training stumbles onto the right Jacobian,
build the structure in. Section~\ref{sec:B-structure} does exactly this, contrasting a soft penalty
that \emph{asks} for volume preservation with a symplectic architecture that \emph{guarantees} it.

\subsection{Structure-preserving world models}\label{sec:B-structure}

Section~\ref{sec:B-mechanism} argued that the cure is to build the missing structure into the model
rather than hope training discovers it. We test this on the chaotic acrobot with three world models
that impose volume structure with increasing force. The first is the plain MLP of
Sections~\ref{sec:B-model}--\ref{sec:B-mechanism}, with no constraint. The second is a
\emph{volume-penalty} MLP, which adds to the next-step loss a soft penalty
$\bigl(\log\lvert\det\,\partial g_\phi/\partial x\rvert\bigr)^2$ pulling the one-step Jacobian toward
unit determinant---an explicit \emph{request} for volume preservation. The third is a
\emph{Hamiltonian neural network} (HNN), which builds it in structurally, through a symplectic
integration step.

The HNN is the structurally honest model. It learns a scalar Hamiltonian $H_\phi(q,p)$ on canonical
coordinates ($q=\theta$, $p=M(\theta)\,\omega$, with $M$ the mass matrix), forms the Hamiltonian flow
$\dot q=\partial H/\partial p$, $\dot p=-\partial H/\partial q$, and advances it with a symplectic Euler
step; angles enter through a $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ embedding to respect the circle topology. For
the acrobot's non-separable Hamiltonian this step is volume-preserving to $O(dt^2)$---its Jacobian has
unit determinant up to that order---so the model conserves phase-space volume essentially by
construction, not by training; measured along the canonical image of the true orbit, its spectrum sums
to $0.001\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, against the true zero. Because $\lambda_1$ is coordinate-invariant, that
same canonical evaluation gives a value directly comparable to the true $(\theta,\omega)$ exponent.

Figure~\ref{fig:structured} reports the learned $\lambda_1$ of each model against the true acrobot
exponent ($\approx 1.14\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ on this orbit). The two MLPs over-amplify, as in
Section~\ref{sec:B-lyap}: the plain MLP gives $2.47\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and the volume-penalty MLP
$3.02\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$---the soft penalty does not close the gap, and here is no better than no penalty
at all. Only the HNN lands near the truth, at $0.87\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$: the sole model that does not
manufacture spurious sensitivity. The lesson is that the constraint must be hard---\emph{asking} for
volume preservation through a penalty is not enough, while \emph{guaranteeing} it through a symplectic
architecture is.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{fig7.png}
  \caption{Learned $\lambda_1$ on the chaotic acrobot for three world models, against the true exponent
  (dashed, $\approx 1.14\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$). The unconstrained MLP ($2.47\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$) and the
  soft volume-penalty MLP ($3.02\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$) over-amplify; only the symplectic HNN
  ($0.87\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$), volume-preserving by construction, lands near the truth---undershooting by
  about $24\%$. Only a hard structural constraint recovers $\lambda_1$.}
  \label{fig:structured}
\end{figure}

The win is qualified. The HNN recovers the right magnitude and the volume structure, but it undershoots
the true exponent by about $24\%$ ($0.87$ against $1.14\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$). Structure preservation is
therefore necessary but not, on its own, sufficient for an exact match---a gap we leave to the
Limitations (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), along with the finite-time, single-seed, and integrator
dependence of these estimates, the true reference itself included.

Part~B thus mirrors Part~A under one exponent. Where Part~A showed that $\lambda_1$ bounds the gradients
a correct simulator yields, Part~B shows that a learned world model is only as faithful as the
$\lambda_1$ it reproduces---and that next-step accuracy does not deliver it. A model can predict the
next state almost perfectly and still misjudge the chaotic sensitivity of the system it represents;
$\lambda_1$ is the diagnostic that exposes the difference, and a structural prior is what repairs it.

\section{Extensions}\label{sec:extensions}
% Cosmos / world models; GR00T / action models; contact & stiff dynamics.
The diagnostics here are deliberately demonstrated on low-dimensional, single-threaded CPU
systems. The natural next steps push them onto GPU hardware and onto the large learned models of
the surrounding Physical-AI stack.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scale to GPU.} Every diagnostic in this paper---the Benettin/QR estimator, the
gradient-gain slope, and the perturbed-rollout SNR---is a sweep or ensemble average over many
\emph{independent} trajectories (initial-condition averaging, the energy sweep, the SNR ensemble).
The outer loop over trajectories, seeds, and sweep points is therefore \emph{embarrassingly
parallel} and maps directly onto the GPU---in the spirit of the thousands of parallel environments
used for large-scale robot learning---with only the per-trajectory time-stepping remaining
sequential. Re-implementing the Warp simulators on \textbf{Newton} (GPU; the production successor
to the removed \texttt{warp.sim}) is the concrete path, and the prerequisite for everything below.

\item \textbf{Cosmos / large world models.} The Part-B fidelity test extends from our small
surrogate to a large video world model such as \textbf{Cosmos}: does it preserve the physical
Lyapunov spectrum, or hallucinate (and wash out) sensitivity? The error-growth diagnostic
(Section~\ref{sec:B-model}) transfers to pixel space as a qualitative probe; the full
Lyapunov-fidelity diagnostic (Section~\ref{sec:B-lyap}) requires recovering physical state
$(\theta,\omega)$ from generated video---a perception front-end---or a latent-space tangent-vector
adaptation, both research problems rather than a front-end swap, and both demanding GPU-scale
compute.

\item \textbf{GR00T / action models.} For a closed-loop policy such as \textbf{GR00T}, the natural
robustness diagnostic is the finite-time Lyapunov exponent of the observation\,$\to$\,action%
\,$\to$\,next-state map, with the policy in a simulation loop (Isaac Lab / Newton) and numerical
(perturbed-rollout) sensitivities, since a large policy with a finite context window is a
non-Markovian, non-autodiff-friendly map.

\item \textbf{Contact and stiff dynamics.} A distinct gradient-pathology regime---non-smooth rather
than chaotic---deliberately outside this conservative CPU study, but the setting where
differentiable-simulator gradients most often break in practice.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Benettin/QR estimator: telescoping and the log-sum}\label{app:qr}

Sections~\ref{sec:bg-lyapunov} and~\ref{sec:m-lyapunov} assert that averaging the
logarithms of the QR diagonals recovers the spectrum; we record here why, since the
running sum is never formed explicitly in the main text.

Let $J_0,\dots,J_{N-1}$ be the one-step Jacobians along an orbit and $Q^{(0)}\in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$ the initial orthonormal frame ($Q^{(0)\top}Q^{(0)}=I_k$). Each
step performs a thin QR factorisation and feeds the resulting frame to the next step,
\begin{equation}
J_t\,Q^{(t)} = Q^{(t+1)}R^{(t+1)},\qquad t=0,\dots,N-1,
\label{eq:qr-step}
\end{equation}
with $Q^{(t+1)}$ orthonormal-columned and $R^{(t+1)}$ upper triangular with positive
diagonal. Substituting~\eqref{eq:qr-step} repeatedly telescopes the rollout Jacobian
$M_N=\prod_{t<N}J_t$ acting on the initial frame,
\begin{equation}
M_N\,Q^{(0)} = J_{N-1}\cdots J_0\,Q^{(0)}
= Q^{(N)}\,\underbrace{R^{(N)}R^{(N-1)}\cdots R^{(1)}}_{\displaystyle \mathcal{R}_N}.
\label{eq:telescope}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:telescope} is itself a thin QR factorisation of $M_N Q^{(0)}$: the
intermediate frames are not cancelled but carried forward as each step's input, and the
entire accumulated rotation collects in the single factor $Q^{(N)}$. Two properties then
isolate the spectrum.

First, $\mathcal{R}_N$ is a product of upper-triangular matrices, hence upper triangular
with diagonal equal to the product of diagonals,
\begin{equation}
(\mathcal{R}_N)_{ii}=\prod_{t=1}^{N} R^{(t)}_{ii}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\ln(\mathcal{R}_N)_{ii}=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\ln R^{(t)}_{ii},
\label{eq:logsum}
\end{equation}
converting the overflow-prone matrix product into the additive running sum the estimator
accumulates---the entire numerical reason for re-orthonormalising at every step rather
than forming $M_N$ once.

Second, $Q^{(N)}$ has orthonormal columns and is therefore an isometry:
$\norm{Q^{(N)}x}=\norm{x}$, its singular values are all one, $\norm{Q^{(N)}}=1$, and so
$\sigma_i(M_N Q^{(0)})=\sigma_i(\mathcal{R}_N)$. It rotates the frame but neither
stretches nor shrinks it, contributing nothing to any growth rate. Dividing
\eqref{eq:logsum} by the elapsed physical time $N\,dt$ and letting $N\to\infty$ gives
\begin{equation}
\lambda_i=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N\,dt}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\ln R^{(t)}_{ii},
\label{eq:lambda-logsum}
\end{equation}
with convergence guaranteed by Oseledets' theorem for a generic $Q^{(0)}$. ``Generic'' is
what the seeded \emph{random} frame of Section~\ref{sec:m-lyapunov} secures: with
probability one the columns of $Q^{(0)}$ avoid the measure-zero subspaces of the Oseledets
filtration that grow more slowly than the top-$k$ rates, so~\eqref{eq:lambda-logsum}
returns $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_k$ rather than a slower set. That the \emph{diagonal}
of $\mathcal{R}_N$ suffices---rather than its singular values---is exactly because the
isometry $Q^{(N)}$ ties the two together.
